Why Does Time Feel Faster as You Get Older?

Ask anyone over thirty: the years keep getting faster. Childhood summers stretched on forever; now it is suddenly August and you are not sure what happened to March. Scientists have a handful of compelling explanations.

1. The proportional theory: each year is a smaller slice

The oldest explanation dates back to the 19th-century philosopher Paul Janet. When you are ten, one year is a tenth of your entire life — a huge, unfamiliar chunk. At fifty, a year is just two percent of it. Against your ever-growing library of memories, each new year is literally a smaller fraction, and the brain judges durations partly by comparison.

2. Memory density: routine compresses, novelty expands

The best-supported modern explanation concerns retrospective time — how long a period feels when you look back on it. Your brain estimates elapsed time partly by how much memorable stuff it recorded. A childhood summer was dense with firsts: new places, new people, new skills. An adult month of commute–work–dinner–bed collapses into a few indistinguishable memories, so it feels like it passed in a blink.

This is the famous vacation effect: a week somewhere new feels long and rich in retrospect, while the week after you get home vanishes. Novelty stretches remembered time; routine shrinks it.

3. Prospective vs. retrospective time

Timing researchers distinguish two systems. Prospective time is duration judged while it passes ("this wait is dragging"). Retrospective time is duration judged from memory afterwards. Interestingly, they often disagree: a boring afternoon drags in the moment but feels tiny in memory, while a packed day flies by but feels substantial looking back. The "life speeds up" feeling is mostly a retrospective phenomenon — memory is doing the compressing.

4. Attention and the pace of adult life

Adults are busy and attention is finite. When your mind is fully occupied, less attention monitors the passage of time, so days slip past unnoticed. Children, with less to occupy them and everything still new, pay far more attention to the raw experience of time passing — one reason waits feel eternal when you are seven.

Can you slow time back down?

You cannot change the calendar, but you can change what your memory records, and that is what "long" years are made of:

  • Inject novelty: new routes, new recipes, new neighborhoods, new skills. Even small novelties add memory markers.
  • Break autopilot: vary routines deliberately — a different walk, a different café, a different evening order.
  • Take mental snapshots: pausing to notice a moment (a two-breath pause works) makes it far more likely to be remembered.
  • Learn something hard: the early, effortful phase of a new skill is maximally memorable — and maximally slow-feeling in retrospect.

Frequently asked questions

Does time physically speed up as we age?

No — the second is the same length at every age. What changes is perception: attention, memory density and the proportional weight of each new year.

Why do boring days drag but boring years fly?

Boredom makes you attend to time passing (so it drags in the moment), but boredom records few memories — so in retrospect it shrinks to almost nothing.

Is there an age when this starts?

Most people notice acceleration in their twenties to thirties, once routines settle in. The effect is about habit and novelty more than any specific birthday.
