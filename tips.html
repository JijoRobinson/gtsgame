10 Tips to Guess Time More Accurately

Your internal clock is trainable. Use these techniques to estimate short durations more precisely and dominate Versus and Teams mode.

1. Count "one one-thousand"

Saying "one one-thousand, two one-thousand..." takes roughly one second per phrase. It's the classic method because it works. Keep a steady rhythm in your head rather than rushing.

2. Anchor with a familiar song

Most people have a song whose tempo they know cold. If its beat is around 120 BPM, each beat is 0.5 seconds — count beats and divide by two for a quick second count.

3. Use your breath

A relaxed resting breath (inhale + exhale) lasts about 4 seconds. Two full breaths ≈ 8 seconds. It's a surprisingly reliable biological anchor.

4. Visualize a clock

Picture a sweep second hand moving around a clock face. Mental imagery engages the same timing circuits used in real perception and can tighten your estimates.

5. Don't fixate on the screen

Staring at the "Running..." indicator makes time feel slower and throws you off. Glance away, relax your eyes, and focus on your internal count.

6. Know the bias: fear vs. boredom

When you're anxious or excited, time feels longer (you'll guess high). When you're bored or distracted, time feels shorter (you'll guess low). Recognize your state and adjust.

7. Practice at the same range

Our rounds use 3–12 seconds. Spend a few minutes a day on Type & Guess at that exact range and your error will shrink fast — your brain calibrates to familiar durations.

8. Split long intervals

For times near 12 seconds, break the count into chunks of 3 or 4 seconds. Counting "1-2-3, 1-2-3, 1-2-3, 1-2-3" is easier to keep accurate than one long count.

9. Stay consistent between rounds

Use the same technique every round. Consistency builds a repeatable feel; switching methods constantly makes it harder to learn from your errors.

10. Review your misses

After each result, note whether you guessed high or low. A consistent pattern — e.g., always 0.5s short — is a bias you can consciously correct next round.

Practice makes permanent

Research on time perception shows that short-interval estimation improves noticeably with regular practice. Five minutes a day for a week can cut your average error in half. The best part? It's genuinely fun.
